**A faster home EV charger isn't always a better installation. Here's how to choose the right charging speed, understand the electrical requirements, and avoid paying for capacity you may never use.**

Buying an electric vehicle is one decision. Figuring out how to charge it at home is another.

Most EV owners quickly discover that Level 2 charging is considerably faster than plugging into a standard 120-volt outlet. What isn't explained nearly as well is that **not all Level 2 charging installations provide the same amount of power.**

A 24-amp charger, a 40-amp charger, and a 48-amp charger all operate on 240 volts, but they have different circuit requirements, charging capabilities, and installation considerations.

And just because a manufacturer advertises a charger capable of 48 amps doesn't mean your vehicle can use all 48 amps — or that you need that much charging power in the first place.

At Top Tier Electrical, I look at EV charger installations from two directions: what the vehicle and driver actually need, and what the home's electrical system can safely support.

The goal isn't automatically to install the biggest circuit possible. It's to design a charging setup that works reliably for your vehicle, your schedule, and your home.

## Quick Answer

- **24A charging:** A practical choice for shorter commutes, plug-in hybrids, or homes where available electrical capacity is limited.
- **30A–32A charging:** A strong middle ground for everyday driving and vehicles with more limited onboard AC charging capability.
- **40A charging:** A versatile option for many fully electric vehicles, providing substantial overnight charging capacity.
- **48A charging:** Worth considering for compatible vehicles, high-mileage driving, shorter charging windows, or future charging needs.
- **The deciding factors:** Your vehicle's onboard charger, daily mileage, overnight parking time, existing electrical capacity, and installation conditions.

All of these can be appropriate when selected and installed correctly.

## Understanding the Different Level 2 Charging Speeds

Level 2 charging uses a 240-volt electrical supply in a typical residential installation.

The difference between charging setups is how much current the equipment can deliver to your vehicle.

Higher current means more available charging power, provided the vehicle can accept it.

### Level 2 Charging Amperage Comparison

| Charging output | Typical circuit breaker | Maximum power at 240V | Approximate range added per hour |
| --------------- | ----------------------- | --------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| 16 amps         | 20 amps                 | 3.8 kW                | 10 miles                         |
| 24 amps         | 30 amps                 | 5.8 kW                | 16 miles                         |
| 30 amps         | 40 amps                 | 7.2 kW                | 19 miles                         |
| 32 amps         | 40 amps                 | 7.7 kW                | 21 miles                         |
| 40 amps         | 50 amps                 | 9.6 kW                | 26 miles                         |
| 48 amps         | 60 amps                 | 11.5 kW               | 31 miles                         |

_Charging-range estimates assume a vehicle averaging 3 miles per kWh and approximately 90% charging efficiency. Actual charging performance depends on the vehicle, battery conditions, electrical supply, and other factors. Circuit ratings are standard examples for continuous EV charging loads; final circuit design depends on the equipment and applicable electrical requirements._

### Why is the breaker larger than the charging amperage?

This is one of the most commonly misunderstood parts of EV charging.

EV charging is generally considered a continuous electrical load.

For typical residential circuits, the maximum continuous charging current is 80% of the circuit breaker's rating.

For example:

- A 30A circuit typically supports 24A charging.
- A 40A circuit typically supports up to 32A charging.
- A 50A circuit typically supports up to 40A charging.
- A 60A circuit typically supports up to 48A charging.

Notice that **30A charging output and a 30A circuit are not the same thing.**

A charger configured to deliver 30A continuously generally requires a 40A circuit, while a 30A circuit generally supports a maximum output of 24A.

Not every charging station offers every amperage setting. Many popular adjustable chargers use 16A, 24A, 32A, 40A, and 48A settings.

The charging equipment must be configured for the actual circuit installed.

## How Much Charging Power Do You Really Need?

Before deciding between a 40A or 48A charger, consider how long your vehicle normally sits at home.

Let's say you drive 40 miles per day.

You get home at 6 PM, plug in, and don't leave again until 7 AM.

That's 13 hours your vehicle could potentially be connected to the charger.

How long would it take to replace those 40 miles?

| Charging output | Approximate time to replace 40 miles |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| 16A             | 3 hours 52 minutes                   |
| 24A             | 2 hours 34 minutes                   |
| 32A             | 1 hour 56 minutes                    |
| 40A             | 1 hour 33 minutes                    |
| 48A             | 1 hour 17 minutes                    |

_Illustrative times based on the same efficiency assumptions used above._

Look at the difference between 40A and 48A.

The 48A setup provides 20% more charging power, but in this example it only saves about 16 minutes.

If the car is parked for 13 hours, that extra performance isn't likely to change your daily routine.

Now consider someone who drives 150 miles a day, works irregular shifts, or regularly needs to recharge between trips.

For that driver, charging speed becomes a much more important factor.

**The question isn't how fast the charging station can charge. It's how fast you actually need your vehicle to charge.**

## Your Vehicle Might Be the Limiting Factor

This is something I would want every homeowner to understand before buying a charging station.

A Level 2 charger installed on the wall is technically electric vehicle supply equipment, or EVSE. The onboard AC charger inside the vehicle determines how much charging power the vehicle can accept.

Some EVs support 48A AC charging. Others are limited to 32A or another maximum, depending on their model and configuration.

Suppose your vehicle can accept only 32A.

Installing a 48A charging station doesn't suddenly make the vehicle charge at 48A.

The vehicle will still limit charging to what its onboard equipment supports.

The higher-capacity installation might be useful for a future vehicle, but it won't provide additional charging speed for the one you currently own.

Before choosing an installation, check the vehicle's year, model, trim, and maximum AC charging rate.

It's one of the first details worth confirming.

## When Does 24A Charging Make Sense?

A 24A Level 2 charger might not sound particularly powerful compared with a 48A model, but it can still replace a substantial amount of daily driving.

At approximately 5.8 kW, a 24A setup provides a meaningful improvement over standard Level 1 charging.

It's worth considering for homeowners who:

- Have relatively predictable commuting distances.
- Park their vehicles overnight.
- Own plug-in hybrids or EVs with modest charging requirements.
- Have limited available electrical capacity.
- Want a permanent Level 2 charging setup without automatically maximizing circuit size.

There's nothing inherently lower-quality about a correctly designed 24A installation.

The materials, equipment, electrical connections, protection, and workmanship should meet the same professional standards expected from a higher-output installation.

The difference is available charging power.

## Why 30A and 32A Charging Deserve More Attention

Many homeowners assume the practical choices are either a 50A circuit or a 60A circuit.

But 30A and 32A charging output can be more than sufficient for everyday use.

At 32A, a vehicle can receive approximately 7.7 kW at 240 volts.

That's enough to replace around 20 miles of range per hour under our example assumptions.

A vehicle parked for eight hours could theoretically add considerably more range than most commuters use in a typical day.

For vehicles that cannot accept more than approximately 32A AC charging, this can also be the point beyond which a larger installation offers no immediate speed advantage.

An important distinction: 30A and 32A charging output generally use the same nominal 40A circuit rating in conventional residential installations.

That means the installation requirements may be very similar, even though the charging station's configured output differs.

If your vehicle supports 32A and the equipment and circuit are rated appropriately, there's often little reason to intentionally limit it to 30A unless another constraint applies.

## Why 40A Charging Is Worth Considering

For many fully electric vehicle owners, 40A charging provides an excellent balance of available power and everyday convenience.

A 40A charging output delivers approximately 9.6 kW at 240 volts.

That is enough to replace a significant amount of driving in just a few hours, making it a practical choice for many households.

It also works with numerous adjustable-output Level 2 charging stations.

### Another consideration for West Michigan homeowners

If your electric utility is Consumers Energy, charging output can affect rebate eligibility.

As of October 2026, Consumers Energy's published residential home-charger installation rebate requirements specify a maximum output of 9.6 kW, with a maximum 50A circuit and 40A charging output.

Choosing a higher-output installation without checking program requirements could affect the incentives available to you.

Utility programs can change, so eligibility should be verified before purchasing equipment or committing to an installation configuration.

## When Is 48A Charging Worth It?

A 48A charging station delivers approximately 11.5 kW at 240 volts and generally requires a dedicated 60A circuit.

It's a legitimate option for homeowners who can use the additional power.

Situations where I would consider it include:

- A vehicle capable of accepting 48A AC charging.
- Regularly driving substantial distances.
- Returning home with limited time before the next departure.
- Larger battery vehicles with demanding daily usage.
- Planning for a future EV with greater charging capability.

But those factors still need to be evaluated alongside the home's electrical capacity.

If installing 48A requires substantial additional electrical work and 40A already covers the owner's driving needs, the additional capacity may provide little everyday benefit.

On the other hand, if a 48A installation fits the electrical system and the customer's long-term plans, it can be a reasonable investment.

Neither option is universally better.

## How Charging Amperage Affects the Electrical Installation

Higher charging output doesn't just change a setting on the charging station.

It also changes what the electrical installation may need to support.

### 1. Circuit breaker and conductor requirements

As charging amperage increases, the branch circuit must be designed for the additional electrical load.

This may affect breaker selection, conductor size, wiring method, and associated installation materials.

However, wire size cannot be selected from charging amperage alone.

The conductor type, installation method, temperature ratings, circuit length, manufacturer requirements, and applicable code provisions all matter.

That's why I don't recommend relying on a universal wire-size chart without considering the actual installation.

### 2. Available panel and service capacity

A higher-amperage charger adds a larger load to the home's electrical system.

But an important point is often overlooked:

**Having a 100-amp electrical service does not automatically mean you cannot install Level 2 EV charging.**

A proper electrical load calculation helps establish what the existing system can support.

Some homes can accommodate a charger without a service upgrade.

Others may benefit from selecting a lower charging output or using a compatible, approved energy management system.

And some installations genuinely require additional electrical improvements.

A service upgrade should be recommended based on the electrical system's condition and calculated requirements — not simply because someone wants to charge an EV.

For more detail, read [Do I Need a Panel Upgrade for an EV Charger?](/blog/do-i-need-a-panel-upgrade-for-an-ev-charger)

### 3. Distance and wiring route

Charging amperage is only part of the installation.

Consider two homes:

**Home A:** The electrical panel is in the garage, and the charger will mount a few feet away.

**Home B:** The electrical panel is on the opposite side of the house, with the charger going into a detached garage.

Even with identical charging equipment and the same amperage, these are very different installations.

Home B may require substantial additional wiring, routing, trenching, or changes to existing electrical infrastructure.

Higher charging amperage can further affect the materials and design, particularly on longer runs.

That's why an accurate installation proposal considers both the desired charging output and the actual conditions of the property.

Our [garage charger placement guide](/blog/where-to-install-ev-charger) explains how location affects both convenience and electrical routing.

### 4. Hardwired versus plug-in charging

Another decision is whether to install a wall-mounted hardwired station or a compatible 240V receptacle.

A properly installed 50A NEMA 14-50 receptacle circuit commonly supports up to 40A continuous EV charging.

A 48A charging setup generally requires hardwired equipment on an appropriately rated 60A circuit.

Hardwiring can also be an excellent option for lower-amperage installations.

For a permanent home charging location, I generally favor a properly designed hardwired installation. It eliminates the additional plug-and-receptacle connection and can provide a clean finished appearance.

However, a properly selected and installed receptacle can make sense for compatible portable equipment.

The correct approach depends on the charging station, installation location, and how the owner plans to use it.

## What Makes a Quality EV Charger Installation?

A charging station can look simple from the outside. There's a box on the wall, a cable, and a vehicle plugged into it.

But the work behind the charging station is what matters over the long term.

EV charging can place a sustained electrical load on a circuit for hours at a time.

The installation needs to be designed with that operating condition in mind.

When evaluating an EV charger installation, I pay attention to more than whether the station turns on.

**Electrical capacity.** Confirm the existing system is suitable for the proposed charging load.

**Proper equipment selection.** Use appropriately certified, compatible charging equipment and electrical components.

**Circuit and conductor design.** Size and install the wiring and overcurrent protection for the actual equipment and operating conditions.

**Electrical connections.** Proper terminations and manufacturer-specified tightening requirements matter, particularly with sustained loads.

**Routing and protection.** Wiring should be installed using an appropriate method, protected against damage, and routed with the finished appearance in mind.

**Charger configuration.** Adjustable charging equipment needs to be commissioned with the correct circuit and output settings.

**Permitting and verification.** Required permits, inspections, labeling, and operational checks belong in a professionally completed scope.

A 24A installation and a 48A installation may have different electrical requirements, but neither should compromise on workmanship.

## Should You Plan for a Future Electric Vehicle?

Future planning is worth discussing, particularly if you're opening finished walls, installing underground wiring, or making other electrical improvements at the same time.

But there's a difference between planning intelligently and automatically installing oversized electrical infrastructure.

Questions worth considering include:

- Do you expect to replace your current EV with a different model?
- Could your household eventually own two electric vehicles?
- Do you plan to install a garage subpanel, workshop equipment, or other significant loads?
- Would adding future wiring be difficult once the current project is finished?
- Could compatible charging equipment share available power between vehicles?

Sometimes installing infrastructure for future expansion makes sense.

Other times, choosing a properly sized charger today and leaving room for future modifications is the more practical option.

The correct answer depends on the property and the owner's plans.

## How I Would Choose the Right Charging Setup

If I were evaluating your home for a Level 2 EV charger, I would start with five questions.

**1. What vehicle are you charging?**

The year, model, and onboard AC charging capability determine how much power the car can actually accept.

**2. How much do you drive?**

Daily mileage and charging habits help determine the power you realistically need.

**3. How long is the vehicle parked at home?**

Someone charging overnight has different requirements than someone needing to recharge between frequent trips.

**4. What can your electrical system support?**

The service, panel, existing loads, and available capacity establish which configurations are appropriate.

**5. What are your long-term plans?**

A second EV, future electrical improvements, or a different vehicle may influence the design.

Once those questions are answered, choosing the charging output becomes much easier.

## Common EV Charging Questions

### Is a 48A charger better than a 40A charger?

Not automatically. A 48A setup provides 20% more maximum charging power, but the benefit depends on whether your vehicle can accept it and whether that additional speed improves your daily charging routine.

### Can I charge an EV with a 30A circuit?

Yes, with properly selected equipment. A conventional 30A branch circuit typically supports a maximum continuous charging output of 24A.

A charger delivering 30A of continuous output generally needs a 40A circuit.

### Can a 100A electrical panel support Level 2 charging?

Possibly. The existing electrical loads, available service capacity, panel condition, and proposed charging output must be evaluated. A 100A service does not automatically require upgrading simply to add EV charging.

### Does a faster charger increase my electric bill?

Charging at higher amperage draws more power while actively charging, but it also finishes charging sooner.

For the same energy delivered to the battery, the overall electricity consumption is generally similar, although equipment efficiency, vehicle heating, and electric rates can affect actual costs.

### Can a charger be turned up to 48A later?

Only if the charging equipment, branch circuit, wiring, protection, available electrical capacity, and commissioning settings support that output.

A setting in an app doesn't increase the actual electrical capacity of an installed circuit.

### Should I buy the charger before calling an electrician?

You can, but it is often better to confirm the vehicle's charging capability, electrical requirements, desired mounting location, and utility program eligibility first.

That helps avoid purchasing equipment that isn't the best fit for your home.

## The Bottom Line: The Right Charger Is the One That Fits Your Home

Level 2 EV charging doesn't have to mean installing the largest charging circuit available.

A properly selected 24A or 32A charger can be an excellent everyday solution. A 40A installation offers substantial charging capability for many vehicles. And 48A can be valuable when the vehicle and driving conditions justify it.

What matters is choosing the appropriate system and installing it correctly.

**A quality EV charger installation isn't defined by how many amps you install. It's defined by the quality of the design, equipment, electrical work, and the reliability of the finished system.**
